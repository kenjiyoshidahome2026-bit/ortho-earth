Six months to the second edition

2026-10-22 · Kenji Yoshida

On 15 April 2026 I made the first commit. Its message was "Workspace setup". Six months later there are nearly 3,500 commits, and on 17 October ortho-earth reached its second edition, 2.0. It feels like a good moment to look back at the road so far.

Spring: the invisible half first

April began with a first map, ortho-map, but what May and June went into was not the map itself. It was the layer underneath: GeoPBF, a container that carries geographic data as shapes, and Gint, which holds those shapes in a form the GPU can read directly. Nothing appeared on screen, so from the outside it looked as if I was doing nothing. Still, I was sure that if I skipped this part I would have to rebuild everything later.

Summer: putting Japan on the globe

In July the PLATEAU buildings went onto the globe. One district at first, then all 300 cities and wards in the dataset. For days I chased the seven districts whose buildings refused to appear, one at a time. I remember well the moment the streets of Tokyo rose up from a round Earth.

On 1 August the drawing moved to WebGPU. Globe, terrain, buildings, Gint and the starry sky all went across in a single day. The next day I spent facing a white screen on an iPhone, building one tool after another to name the cause, until it finally ran on the real device. That same day I decided WebGPU would be the default. Older devices still get WebGL2.

In the same month came ortho-solar, a true-scale Solar System, and a visualisation of Japan's 2020 census. A map does not have to stop at the surface of the Earth, and statistics do not have to stay in tables.

Autumn: to the world, and to compatibility

September was the month a map of Japan became a map of the world. The interface went into 26 languages, and you can now move back and forth between the round globe and Equal Earth, a flat map of the whole world. I rebuilt the top page of www and put these words at the top: "Equal to all, fair to the Earth." The Earth is round, and maps belong to everyone. I wanted to say that first.

At the end of the month I cut the globe out as its own package, @ortho-earth/globe, and made it usable with the same code as MapLibre GL JS. The yardstick was MapLibre's own gallery of 139 official examples: each one runs side by side with the real MapLibre, and the picture and the answers are graded. At first not a single example drew the same picture. Today 66 draw the same picture, 50 give the same answers, and none fail to run. I did not want people to learn a new API. I wanted the code they already know to run, unchanged, on a round Earth. That was the thing I wanted most.

What 2.0 threw away

In a word, the second edition is a rewrite and a clean-up. Every library is now written in TypeScript (strict), and the old names and back doors that 1.x kept for compatibility are closed. It is a slightly unkind change for users, but the rule in this workshop is that when you move into a new form, you throw the old one away. What changed is set out in detail in "Ortho Earth 2.0 is out".

After 2.0

Right after the release I measured the engine against four others. Measuring turned up weaknesses I had not noticed myself: on iPhone it was holding back its downloads too much, the screen flashed white for an instant when the camera stopped, and its memory accounting was too loose. Within a few days they were fixed, in 2.0.2 and in the next release. That story is in "Rendering PLATEAU 3D city models: MapLibre, Cesium, TerriaJS, Navara and ortho-earth compared".

Looking back

Six months ago I did not imagine I would get this far. I have not written a single line of the code. What I did every day was look at the screen and say "that's wrong", and say "fix the root cause". Even so, pile up 3,500 small decisions and it really does become a map, and I am a little surprised by that.

2.0 is not an ending. The foundations have only just set. From here on I intend to put on this Earth the things I truly want to draw.

Kenji Yoshida @ Yokohama
